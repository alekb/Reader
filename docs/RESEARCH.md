# Teaching an 8-year-old late starter to read English: evidence-based design brief

Scope: a child of 8 who knows only part of the alphabet and cannot yet read. The brief covers what the research says, then turns it into an implementable specification (GPC sequence, word lists, tricky-word order, decodable sentences, session structure, mastery/spacing rules). Sources are listed inline and in section 11. Note on access: the research was done through web search; several primary pages (Reading Rockets, EEF, Oxford Owl, Jolly Learning) could not be fetched directly from this environment, so some details are taken from search summaries of those pages and are flagged where confidence is lower.

---

## 1. Why structured literacy / systematic synthetic phonics (SSP)

### 1.1 The evidence base

- **National Reading Panel (2000)** reviewed ~100,000 studies (c. 400-500 met its methodological bar) and identified five essential components: phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, comprehension. Key findings for this project:
  - *Systematic* phonics (a planned sequence of letter-sound correspondences) outperformed incidental/opportunistic phonics.
  - Phonics benefits were found from kindergarten through 6th grade and for children struggling to read, so an 8-year-old non-reader is squarely inside the population the evidence covers.
  - Inadequate phonemic awareness underlies many reading difficulties; explicit PA instruction improved reading and spelling.
  - Sources: [Reading Rockets, Findings of the NRP](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/curriculum-and-instruction/articles/findings-national-reading-panel); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Reading_Panel). A caveat from [Natalie Wexler](https://nataliewexler.substack.com/p/is-it-time-to-topple-the-five-pillars): the PA and phonics findings are solid; the "five pillars" infographic overstates the comprehension findings and underplays background knowledge.
- **IES/What Works Clearinghouse practice guide, *Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding K-3*** (2016, rev. 2019) gives four recommendations with evidence levels: (1) academic language (minimal evidence), (2) phonemic awareness and linking sounds to letters (**strong**), (3) decoding, word analysis, writing and recognizing words (**strong**), (4) reading connected text daily for accuracy, fluency and comprehension (moderate), with the explicit note that "as soon as students can decode even a few words, they should practice reading connected text." Sources: [IES guide (epub)](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_070516.epub); [PDF mirror](https://www.galiteracycenter.org/s/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf).
- **Ehri's phase model** explains why decoding has to be taught, not guessed. Readers move through pre-alphabetic (logos/pictures), partial alphabetic (first letter + guess), full alphabetic (every grapheme mapped to a phoneme), consolidated alphabetic (chunks: digraphs, blends, syllables, morphemes). Orthographic mapping, the process that bonds a word's spelling, pronunciation and meaning in memory for instant "sight" retrieval, is enabled by phonemic awareness plus grapheme-phoneme knowledge. An 8-year-old who knows part of the alphabet and cannot read is in the pre-alphabetic/partial-alphabetic phase; the whole programme is about getting him to full alphabetic as fast as possible. Sources: [UFLI summary of Ehri's phases](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/EhriPhases.pdf); [Reading Rockets summary of Ehri 2014](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/orthographic-mapping-acquisition-sight-word-reading-spelling-memory-and); [Lexia on alphabetic phase theory](https://lexialearning.com/blog/alphabetic-phase-theory-4-steps-to-literacy-success).
- **EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit (UK)**: phonics averages about +4 months' additional progress and is "consistently found to be effective in supporting younger readers." It adds the caution that for pupils aged **10+** who have not succeeded with phonics, other approaches (comprehension strategies, metacognition) may be more successful, and that more research is needed on older pupils. Source: [EEF phonics summary (PDF mirror)](https://images.scholastic.co.uk/assets/a/5c/7d/eef-phonics-1619193.pdf); [EEF Fresh Start evaluation news](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-publishes-independent-evaluation-of-read-write-inc-phonics-and-fresh-start). An 8-year-old non-reader is below that 10+ threshold and has not "failed" phonics (he has never had it), so SSP remains the first-line approach.

### 1.2 How an older beginner differs from a 5-year-old

| Dimension | 5-year-old beginner | 8-year-old late starter | Design implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oral language, vocabulary, working memory | Still developing | Much stronger | Can hold 3-4 phonemes in memory sooner; can learn 1-2 GPCs per session instead of 1 per day; can learn the *logic* ("this letter usually says /a/") explicitly |
| Self-image | No notion of being "behind" | Often shame, embarrassment, "I don't care" defence; acutely aware of peers | Private practice, age-appropriate art/characters, visible progress, no babyish materials ([Pennington](https://blog.penningtonpublishing.com/tag/reading-intervention/page/4/); [Sunshine Books](https://www.sunshine.co.nz/understanding-the-challenges/)) |
| Guessing habits | None yet | May have learned to guess from pictures/first letter (partial-alphabetic strategies) | Use decodable text with minimal picture cues; use nonsense words (as Toe by Toe does) to make guessing impossible ([Structural Learning on Toe by Toe](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/toe-by-toe)) |
| Pace | Slow, lots of repetition | Gaps "can be efficiently and quickly filled" ([Curriculum Associates](https://www.curriculumassociates.com/blog/strategies-for-struggling-readers)) | Mastery-based progression: advance as soon as criteria are met, don't pace by calendar |
| Need for predictability | Enjoys novelty | Predictable routine lets older readers "let down their guard and trust the process" ([Collaborative Classroom](https://collaborativeclassroom.org/blog/helping-striving-readers-high-school)) | Same lesson skeleton every day; only the content changes |
| Content | Cat/dog/pig stories are fine | Needs plots and humour that don't feel like a toddler book | Catch-up decodable series (Phonic Books Moon Dogs / Magic Belt / That Dog!, ages 8-14) exist precisely for this ([Phonic Books](https://phonicbooks.co.uk/pages/catch-up-phonics-series-for-struggling-readers)) |

## 2. Letter-sound (GPC) teaching order

### 2.1 Sounds before names, and pure sounds

- **Sounds first.** Reading works by sounds, not names. Research is thin and the emerging consensus is that names and sounds can be taught together ([Shanahan](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/should-we-teach-letter-names)), but SSP programmes lead with sounds because a child taught names first expects vowels to say their names and reads "got" as "goat" ([Phonics Hero](https://phonicshero.com/teach-letter-names/)). Letter-name knowledge is a strong predictor of later decoding (NELP meta-analysis of 52 studies, via Shanahan) and names help retain sounds when the name contains the sound (Share 2004). **Recommendation for this learner:** he already knows some names; keep them, but every card/screen prompt asks "what SOUND?", and names are used only when discussing spellings ("the letters s and h together say /sh/").
- **Pure sounds, no schwa.** Say /m/ as "mmm", not "muh"; /t/ as a short clipped "t", not "tuh". "muh-a-tuh" blends to something far from "mat"; "mmmaaat" blends to "mat" ([Phonics Hero, Pronouncing the Sounds](https://phonicshero.com/pronouncing-the-sounds/); [Monster Phonics](https://monsterphonics.com/the-importance-of-pure-sounds-in-phonics/)). Continuous sounds (s, m, n, f, l, r, v, z, sh, th) can be stretched; stop sounds (t, p, k, b, d, g) must be clipped. Any audio in an app must be recorded this way.

### 2.2 The sequence (UK *Letters and Sounds* 2007 / Jolly Phonics-style)

The order is deliberately non-alphabetical so that words can be built after a handful of letters; Jolly Learning's teacher guide notes that the first group alone (s a t i p n) builds "sat, pin, tap" and gives early success in reading and writing ([Jolly Learning Group 1](https://jollylearning.com/resources/jolly-phonics/teachers-guide/group-1); [St Julian's Primary](https://www.stjuliansprimary.com/jolly-phonics)). The sets also separate visually confusable letters (b/d, p/q, m/n are never introduced together) and front-load high-frequency, continuous sounds (s, a, t, n, m).

## 3. Blending, segmenting and phoneme manipulation

### 3.1 Oral (no letters) first, then with letters
Letters and Sounds Phase 1 Aspect 7 (oral blending/segmenting) uses "robot talk"/"sound-talk": the adult says "put on your c-oa-t", "I spy a c-u-p", the child blends; then reverses it so the child segments. Source: [Leicestershire Phase 1/2 examples (PDF)](https://resources.leicestershire.gov.uk/sites/resource/files/field/pdf/2017/1/12/letter_and_sounds_phase_1_and_2_examples.pdf); [Townhill parent guide](https://files.schudio.com/townhill-infant-school-2/files/documents/Parent_Guide_to_Phase_1_Phonics.pdf). For an 8-year-old this needs only a few minutes per session as a warm-up, not weeks of pre-work, and should run alongside Phase 2 letter teaching rather than before it (the IES guide's strong-evidence recommendation is to link phonemic awareness to letters).

### 3.2 Blending technique: continuous ("connected phonation")
- **Gonzalez-Frey & Ehri (2020/21, *Scientific Studies of Reading*)**: 38 kindergartners who could not decode nonwords were taught either *segmented* phonation ("sss - aaa - nnn", pauses) or *connected* phonation ("sssaaannn", no pauses). Connected phonation produced better transfer to harder stop-consonant words and the advantage held at delayed post-test; segmented-phonation learners more often forgot the initial sounds by the time they reached the end. Source: [Reading Rockets summary](https://www.readingrockets.org/research-by-topic/connected-phonation-more-effective-segmented-phonation-teaching-beginning-readers); [Collaborative Classroom](https://www.collaborativeclassroom.org/blog/the-power-of-continuous-blending-using-connected-phonation-to-support-decoding/); [PLD Literacy review](https://pld-literacy.org/continuous-or-discontinuous-blending-for-early-readers-what-does-the-evidence-say/).
- **Practical ordering (ECRI / Colorado DoE routines)**: begin with words that *start* with continuous sounds (sun, man, fin, sat), then add stop sounds at the end (mat, sad), and only later stop sounds at the start (tap, pig, dog). Source: [Colorado DoE ECRI foundational skills](https://www.cde.state.co.us/coloradoliteracy/ecrifoundationalskillssection7). Amplify adds: for stop sounds use quick iteration ("t-t-t") rather than holding; shorter gaps between phonemes make the word easier to hear. Source: [Amplify K skills map](https://rebuild.prod.amplify.com/remotelearning/mclass/kindergarten-skills-map/rf-1-2-b-i-orally-produce-vc-cv-words-by-blending-two-phonemes).
- **Successive blending** as a scaffold when the child cannot hold three sounds: blend the first two ("sa"), then add the third ("sa-t" -> "sat"). Source: [What I Have Learned Teaching](https://whatihavelearnedteaching.com/successive-blending-intervention/).
- **"Say it slow / say it fast" (Direct Instruction, *Reading Mastery* and *Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons*)**: the teacher stretches the word ("mmmaaannn"), then cues "say it fast" and the child collapses it to "man"; if the child answers slowly, the teacher models the fast version and has the child repeat. Source: [100 Easy Lessons overview](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/100-easy-lessons-revised/34100176); [Reading Mastery scope and sequence (PDF)](https://www.mheducation.com/unitas/school/program/reading-mastery-signature-edition-2008/scope-and-sequence/rmse-scope-and-sequence-reading-gk.pdf).
- Read Write Inc calls the same thing "Fred Talk" (the puppet can only speak in sounds) and spelling "Fred Fingers" (press one sound onto each finger). Source: [RWI glossary (PDF)](https://www.newinf.cumbria.sch.uk/_site/data/files/migrated/reading/read-write-inc-glossary.pdf).

### 3.3 Segmenting (for spelling) and manipulation
- Segmenting is the reverse of blending and is taught in the same session: say the word, "chop" it into sounds on fingers, write/select one grapheme per sound (sound boxes / Elkonin boxes; RWI "Fred Fingers").
- Phoneme manipulation (deletion, substitution) is a later-developing skill (typically not mastered until ~3rd grade) and is the basis of Kilpatrick's "One Minute Activities" (e.g. "Say *spy*, but instead of /p/ say /k/" -> sky). Useful as quick oral warm-ups once blending/segmenting are secure, but a critique (Bell 2023) notes that oral-only PA training should not displace reading practice with print. Sources: [Ohio DoE advanced PA (PDF)](https://education.ohio.gov/getattachment/Topics/Learning-in-Ohio/Literacy/Striving-Readers-Comprehensive-Literacy-Grant1/2020-Literacy-Academy/3-04-4-04-Advanced-PA-ACCESSIBLE-PDF.pdf.aspx?lang=en-US); [Nomanis critique (PDF)](https://info.multilit.com/hubfs/Nomanis-15%202023/NOMANIS15-SEP23-BELL.pdf).

**Manipulation activity ladder (oral, 1 min each):**
1. Blend: "/s/ /u/ /n/ -> ?" 2. Segment: "sun -> /s/ /u/ /n/" 3. Isolate: "first sound in sun?" 4. Delete: "sun without /s/?" (un) 5. Substitute: "sun, change /s/ to /r/" (run) 6. Add: "at, put /m/ in front" (mat).

## 4. High-frequency and "tricky" words: the heart-word method

- Most high-frequency words are fully decodable once the relevant GPCs are known (at, in, it, and, can). Only the genuinely irregular ones (or ones with a not-yet-taught GPC) should be taught as "tricky"/"heart" words, and even those are *mapped*, not memorised as shapes. Source: [Reading Rockets, Sight words and orthographic mapping](https://www.readingrockets.org/reading-101/reading-and-writing-basics/sight-words-and-orthographic-mapping); [What I Have Learned Teaching, Heart Word Method](https://whatihavelearnedteaching.com/heart-word-method-mapping-sight-words/).
- **Routine (Reading Rockets "Teaching Heart Words", modelled on *said*):** (1) say the word, use it in a sentence; (2) stretch/tap the sounds and count them (said = /s/ /e/ /d/, 3 sounds); (3) draw one line per sound; (4) ask which letter(s) spell each sound and write them in; (5) mark the regular parts (s, d) and put a heart over the part to "learn by heart" (ai = /e/); (6) read it, spell it aloud, write it, read it in a sentence. Source: [Reading Rockets Teaching Heart Words (PDF)](https://www.readingrockets.org/sites/default/files/migrated/teaching-heart-words.pdf); [Heart Word Magic](https://www.readingrockets.org/node/34202); [Lucky Little Learners](https://luckylittlelearners.com/?p=210767).
- A word stops being a "heart word" once its pattern is taught (e.g. *like* is tricky in Phase 4 but decodable after i-e in Phase 5).

**Tricky-word order (one or two per set, introduced only after the child can read the decodable words of that set):**

1. Phase 2: **the, to, I, no, go, into**
2. Phase 3: **he, she, we, me, be, was, my, you, they, her, all, are**
3. Phase 4: **said, so, have, like, some, come, were, there, little, one, do, when, out, what**
4. Phase 5: **oh, their, people, Mr, Mrs, looked, called, asked, could, water, where, who, again, thought, through, work, mouse, many, laughed, because, different, any, eyes, friends, once, please**

Heart-part notes for the first twelve: the (th regular, e = /u/ heart); to (t regular, o = /oo/ heart); I (whole word heart); no/go (n/g regular, o = /oa/ heart); into (in + to); he/she/we/me/be (consonant regular, e = /ee/ heart); was (w regular, a = /o/, s = /z/); my (m regular, y = /igh/); you (y regular, ou = /oo/).

## 5. Decodable text

### 5.1 Why
- Decodable texts contain only the GPCs and tricky words taught so far; they let the child practise blending in connected text and build the "reading reflex" of using letters first rather than pictures or guessing. Mesmer (2005) found children using decodables applied phonics more, read more accurately and needed less help. A review on Reading Rockets concludes decodability is a critical characteristic of early text mainly for accuracy, and that it should be one criterion among several. Sources: [Reading Rockets, What are decodable books](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/curriculum-and-instruction/articles/what-are-decodable-books-and-why-are-they-important); [review of evidence](https://readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/influence-decodability-early-reading-text-reading-achievement-review); [Shanahan, Should we teach decodable text](https://www.readingrockets.org/blogs/shanahan-on-literacy/should-we-teach-decodable-text).
- Caveats: the window is short (Mesmer 2010); once the child decodes fluently, move to ordinary books. A UC study found decodables that are too hard still fail struggling beginners, so the text must track the learner's *actual* taught set. Source: [eScholarship study](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/3824429q). For an 8-year-old the decodable text window may be only a few months, which is fine: the point is to get through it quickly.

**Phase 3 later (vowel digraphs/trigraphs; tricky: her all are):** The moon is bright at night. / The goat is on the road. / Keep the boat in the shed. / Mark had a hard day at the farm. / Turn the car at the fork. / Now the cow is down in the town. / Join the coins in a pair. / I can hear the owl at the fair. / The hammer is in the shed. / Are you sure? Yes, I am.
**Phase 4 (adjacent consonants; tricky: said so have like some come were there little one do when out what):** Fred went to the tent with a lamp. / The frog can jump and swim. / "Stop that truck!" said Mum. / I have a little drum. / There is a crisp in my lunchbox. / Can you help me lift the milk? / When we got there the shop was shut. / Some of the best things are free. / The twins went to the windmill. / What is that in the sand?

Picture rule: in early sentences use no picture, or a picture that does *not* reveal the sentence, so the child cannot guess.

### 5.3 Progression
Single words -> 2-3 word captions -> sentences (5-8 words) -> 3-5 sentence "mini-stories" -> short chapter-style decodables with age-appropriate plots (e.g. the Phonic Books catch-up series: Moon Dogs Set 1 = CVC level "for Year 3 and beyond", Magic Belt / That Dog! = CVC through adjacent consonants, digraphs and suffixes; Totem for later stages). Source: [Phonic Books catch-up](https://phonicbooks.co.uk/pages/catch-up-phonics-series-for-struggling-readers); [SPELD SA guide to decodables](https://speldsa.org.au/blogs/blog/help-choosing-decodable-books). Reviews note some catch-up series lean on peril/monster themes; preview for fit.

## 6. Gamification and practice-design principles for a struggling reader

| Principle | Evidence / source | Implementation |
|---|---|---|
| Short, daily sessions | Toe by Toe: 20 min daily 1:1, programme ~5-6 months ([Structural Learning](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/toe-by-toe); [Stoneyholme school](https://www.stoneyholme.lancsngfl.ac.uk/toe-by-toe/)). Reading Recovery: 30 min daily, 12-20 weeks ([RR Council facts](https://readingrecovery.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/GetTheFacts.pdf); [WWC study page](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/study/77725)). Shanahan: across 18 studies the daily phonics dose ran 15-60 min, median/mode 30 ([Shanahan](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/how-much-phonics-should-i-teach)). Letters and Sounds: daily 20-min discrete session ([Ladybarn](https://www.ladybarn.manchester.sch.uk/curriculum-phonics)). GraphoLearn RCT: 6-week game-based intervention improved word reading in 2nd graders with persistent difficulties ([Ronimus et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-019-09658-3)). | 10-15 min in-app daily is a realistic floor; aim for 15-20 min total including a short read-aloud. Daily beats longer-but-rarer. |
| Dose matters (more hours, more gain) | Donnelly, Huber & Yeatman 2019: 160 h over 8 weeks produced linear dose-response growth; decoding d = 0.85 ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01900/text)). | Track cumulative minutes; make streaks visible. |
| Immediate corrective feedback, errorless-by-design | Direct Instruction: materials designed so ~70% of first-time responses are correct; every error corrected immediately with model-lead-test, then re-tested later in the lesson ([KU Special Connections](https://specialconnections.ku.edu/instruction/direct_instruction); [Carnine et al.](https://newlearningonline.com/literacies/chapter-4/carnine-et-al.-on-direct-instruction)). Edmark uses errorless learning with faded prompts ([STAR Autism Support](https://starautismsupport.com/node/241)). | On a wrong answer: app immediately shows/says the correct sound ("This is /sh/. Say /sh/."), the child repeats, then the item reappears 3-5 items later. Never a buzzer, never a "lives lost" mechanic. Target 80-90% success rate per session by controlling new-vs-review ratio. |
| Spaced / expanding retrieval | Fritz et al. 2007 (preschoolers): half the benefit from spacing, half from retrieval; expanding schedule beat massed ([Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2022/2/3-1)). Leonard et al. 2024 (children with DLD): short early spacing helps, then equal and expanding converge ([JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2024_JSLHR-23-00528)). Evidence is on word learning, not letter sounds, so this is an extrapolation. | Leitner-style boxes per GPC and per tricky word: new -> same session (x3 spread out) -> next day -> 2 days -> 4 -> 7 -> 14 -> 30. Error demotes the item one box. |
| Mastery-based progression, cumulative review | Toe by Toe's cumulative, repetitive structure; RWI "speed sounds" recap at the start of every lesson; Letters and Sounds "revisit and review" segment. | Never drop old items; every session opens with a 60-90 s speed-sounds sweep of everything learned. |
| Gamification has small-to-moderate effects | Sailer & Homner 2020 meta-analysis: g = .49 cognitive, .36 motivational, .25 behavioural; cognitive effect robust to study quality ([Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). GraphoLearn: *cognitive* engagement predicted gains; *emotional* engagement predicted more play time but not more learning ([Ronimus 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-019-09658-3)). | Game elements should wrap the decoding task, not replace it. Reward *completion and effort* (session done, streak kept, items attempted), not accuracy, so errors stay low-stakes. Keep decorations off the word/letter display. |
| Mascot / character | DI's "Fred" puppet who can only talk in sounds (RWI), Letters and Sounds' robot "Metal Mike" for sound-talk ([Leicestershire PDF](https://resources.leicestershire.gov.uk/sites/resource/files/field/pdf/2017/1/12/letter_and_sounds_phase_1_and_2_examples.pdf)). | A mascot who "can only speak in sounds" gives a non-embarrassing reason to segment aloud; it should look like something an 8-year-old would choose (robot, dog, alien), not a nursery character. |
| Predictable routine | Older readers trust a scripted, repetitive structure ([Collaborative Classroom](https://collaborativeclassroom.org/blog/helping-striving-readers-high-school)). | Same 6-step lesson every day. |

## 7. Multisensory (Orton-Gillingham-style) elements

- **What the evidence says:** Stevens et al. 2021 meta-analysis (16 studies, K-12 students with word-reading difficulties): OG interventions had positive but *non-significant* effects on foundational skills (ES = 0.22, p = .40) and on vocabulary/comprehension (0.14), with low study quality. Hall et al. 2022 (53 studies) found cheaper, ordinary explicit phonics interventions were as effective for children with dyslexia. Sources: [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8497161); [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-leading-dyslexia-treatment-isnt-a-magic-bullet-studies-show-while-other-options-show-promise/); [Reading Rockets summary](https://readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/current-state-evidence-examining-effects-orton-gillingham-reading). The active ingredient is explicit, systematic, cumulative phonics; the multisensory add-ons are engagement and memory aids, not magic.
- **Elements worth keeping (cheap, no downside):**
  1. *See-hear-say-write* for each new GPC: look at the grapheme, hear the pure sound, say it, trace it (finger on screen, sand, or in the air with the whole arm) while saying the sound.
  2. *Keyword picture + action*: Jolly Phonics pairs each of 42 sounds with an action (e.g. weave hand like a snake for /s/) ([Jolly Learning](https://jollylearning.com/en-us/our-programs/jolly-phonics)). Ehri's earlier work on integrated picture mnemonics (letter shape embedded in the keyword picture, e.g. the *s* drawn as a snake) is the research basis for this; the exact citation (Ehri, Deffner & Wilce 1984) was not retrievable in this session and is given from memory.
  3. *Finger-tapping/segmenting* (RWI "Fred Fingers").
  4. *Letter tiles / drag-and-drop* for building words during the spelling step, so the child physically moves graphemes.
  5. *Nonsense words* (Toe by Toe) sprinkled into blending practice to force true decoding.
- For an 8-year-old, keep the action/mnemonic phase short (a few exposures) and fade it; he will not want to do "snake arms" for long.

## 9. Handling the older learner: dignity, catch-up programmes, dose data

### 9.1 Dignity and motivation
- Acknowledge shame directly but briefly; frame as "nobody showed you the code yet", not "you are behind".
- Privacy: practice 1:1 or in-app, not in front of siblings/peers.
- Materials: no nursery art; older-looking fonts and characters; chapter-style decodables with real plots ([Phonic Books](https://phonicbooks.co.uk/pages/catch-up-phonics-series-for-struggling-readers)); check that "resources match the age of your students" ([Pennington](https://blog.penningtonpublishing.com/tag/reading-intervention/page/4/)).
- Make progress visible and concrete: a map of the 44 sounds that fills in, a count of words he can now read (after Set 2 alone he can read ~40 words; after Phase 2 ~200).
- Use his stronger oral language: explain *why* ("two letters, one sound"), let him predict, give him agency (choose the mascot, choose which review deck first).
- Predictable, scripted routine so the only novelty is the content.
- Keep sessions short and stop on a success.

### 9.2 Catch-up programmes and what their evidence says

| Programme | Target | Format | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Toe by Toe** (Cowling, 1993) | Weak decoders of any age, incl. dyslexia | 1:1, 20 min daily, strictly cumulative, uses nonsense words, each item ticked 3 times correct on 3 separate days before moving on; ~5-6 months to complete; no training needed | Practitioner/publisher evidence only; no RCT found ([Structural Learning](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/toe-by-toe); [Stoneyholme](https://www.stoneyholme.lancsngfl.ac.uk/toe-by-toe/); [Pearson](https://www.pearson.com/en-au/schools/primary/literacy/toe-by-toe-literacy-program/)) |
| **Read Write Inc. Fresh Start** (Ruth Miskin) | Years 5-8 (ages 9-13) struggling readers | Daily group sessions; speed sounds -> Fred Talk green words -> red words -> modules with age-appropriate texts; must master Set 1 sounds before modules | EEF efficacy trial (10 schools): +3 months. EEF effectiveness trial (131 schools, AIR): -2 months overall, -3 months FSM, but 35% of schools never delivered and 29% delivered partially; EEF says interpret with caution ([EEF project page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/read-write-inc-and-fresh-start); [EEF news](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-publishes-independent-evaluation-of-read-write-inc-phonics-and-fresh-start); [Schools Week](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/education-endowment-foundation-phonics-read-write-inc-fresh-start-study/)) |
| **Sound Discovery / Snappy Lesson** (Marlynne Grant) | Y2-5 | Fast-paced multisensory synthetic phonics lessons | Interventions for Literacy database rates it "substantial" for comprehension Y2-5; no peer-reviewed RCT found ([IfL](https://www.interventionsforliteracy.org.uk/home/interventions/list-view/sound-discovery/)) |
| **Rapid Phonics** | Y6/7 below Level 4b | 6+6 weeks | EEF trial: no significant effect on comprehension; small non-significant decoding gain; heavy disruption at transition ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/rapid-phonics)) |
| **Reading Recovery** (Clay) | Lowest 20% of first graders (age 6) | 1:1, 30 min daily, 12-20 weeks; rereading familiar books, letter/word work, writing a sentence, new book | WWC: positive/potentially positive effects on 4 domains, from only ~5 qualifying studies, ~700 first-graders ([WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/study/77725)). Heavily criticised (Chapman & Tunmer 2015/16) for cueing-based method, excluding the weakest pupils and weak original designs ([Chapman & Tunmer response](https://readingrecovery.org/images/pdfs/Reading_Recovery/Research_and_Evaluation/chapman_tunmer_2015_response.pdf); [Windward](https://www.thewindwardschool.org/the-windward-institute/the-beacon/article/~board/beacon-archives/post/requiem-for-reading-recovery)). Not a model to copy for the *method*; its 30-min-daily 1:1 intensity is the useful takeaway. |
| **Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons** (Engelmann) | Parent-delivered beginners | 20-30 min scripted daily lessons; sound practice, oral blending ("say it slow/say it fast"), word reading, sentence/passage reading | DI generally has a large (if contested) evidence base; WWC found no qualifying studies for its early-reading outcomes ([WWC DI report](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/InterventionReports/WWC_Direct_Instruction_052107.pdf)) |

Takeaways for the design: (a) the 1:1, *daily*, 20-30 minute, cumulative, mastery-gated format recurs across every programme; (b) the negative EEF results for Fresh Start / Rapid Phonics are for 10-13-year-olds in disrupted school settings with poor implementation, not for a motivated daily 1:1 learner of 8; (c) older-learner synthesis (Wanzek/Vaughn, grades 4-12) finds modest effects (0.10-0.16) and no clear advantage for more hours, which argues for making every session count rather than simply lengthening it ([Meadows Center](https://meadowscenter.org/?p=808)).

### 9.3 Daily practice amount: the data
- 15-20 min daily focused phonics is the floor used by Toe by Toe (20), Letters and Sounds (20), 100 Easy Lessons (20-30), Reading Recovery (30), Shanahan's median across studies (30).
- Donnelly et al. 2019: linear dose-response up to 160 h in 8 weeks (4 h/day) in 6-12-year-olds, so more is better if tolerated, but that was a clinic programme; for a home/app learner, consistency (daily) matters more than session length.
- A six-week, game-based letter-sound/word-reading programme produced measurable word-reading gains in struggling 2nd graders (Ronimus et al. 2019).
- Practical recommendation: one 10-15 min app session every day + 5-10 min reading a decodable aloud to an adult, 6-7 days a week; a second short review session (speed sounds only, 3 min) later in the day accelerates automaticity and costs little.

## 10. Implementable specification

### 10.1 Session structure (12-15 minutes, same order every day)

| Step | Time | Content | Rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Speed sounds review | 2 min | Flash every GPC and tricky word due today per the spacing schedule (typically 10-20 items). Child says the sound/word; tap to reveal. | Responses under ~2 s count as automatic. Errors -> model-lead-test, item requeued for later in this session and demoted one box. |
| 2. Sound-talk warm-up | 1 min | 4-6 oral items: mascot says "c-a-t", child blends; child segments a word for the mascot; one manipulation item once Phase 3 begins. | No letters on screen. |
| 3. New GPC (or consolidation day) | 2-3 min | Grapheme + pure sound + keyword picture/action; child says it, traces it (3x), finds it among 3-4 distractors, finds it in 2-3 words. | Max 1 new GPC per session in Phase 2 (2 allowed if yesterday's review accuracy >= 90%). Every 4th session is consolidation: no new GPC, extra blending. |
| 4. Blend words | 3 min | 8-10 words: ~4 with the new GPC, ~4 review, 1-2 nonsense words. Connected phonation modelled on first miss; "say it fast" cue. Successive blending scaffold if child stalls. | Advance to sentences in the same session only if >= 80% words correct. |
| 5. Tricky/heart word | 1 min | Heart-word mapping routine for the current tricky word (or a review one). | One new tricky word per 2-3 sessions, never on the same day as a hard new GPC. |
| 6. Read sentences | 2-3 min | 2-3 decodable sentences using today's words. First read: decode; second read: "read it like talking". One meaning question ("Who sat?"). | Sentences generated only from mastered GPCs + taught tricky words. |
| 7. Spell/build | 2 min | 2-3 words: say word, tap sounds on fingers, drag tiles into sound boxes. | Tiles limited to taught graphemes + 1-2 distractors. |
| 8. Close | 1 min | Progress screen: sounds mastered, words unlocked, streak, effort reward. | Reward never depends on accuracy. End on a success item. |

### 10.2 Mastery and spacing rules

**Per-item (GPC or tricky word) state machine (Leitner boxes):**
- Box 0 (new): presented 3 times within the introducing session, spread across steps 1, 4 and 6.
- Box 1: review next day. Box 2: +2 days. Box 3: +4 days. Box 4: +7 days. Box 5: +14 days. Box 6: +30 days (maintenance).
- Promote on a correct, automatic (< 2 s) response; a slow-but-correct response stays in the same box; an error demotes one box and the item is re-presented 3-5 items later in the same session (DI retest).
- "Mastered" = correct on 3 consecutive scheduled reviews on 3 different days (the Toe by Toe criterion) *and* in box >= 3.

**Set/phase gating:**
- Introduce the next GPC only when >= 85% of the review sweep (step 1) is automatic and no more than 2 items are in box 0-1.
- Move to the next set's word list when blending accuracy on the current set is >= 90% over two consecutive sessions, including nonsense words.
- Move from Phase 2 to Phase 3 when all 19 GPCs are mastered, CVC blending >= 90%, and the 6 tricky words are automatic.
- Phase 4 entry: all 44 GPCs mastered; Phase 5 entry: CVCC/CCVC >= 90%.
- Fluency check (optional, weekly): 1-minute sounds-per-minute and words-per-minute probes, graphed, so progress is visible to the child. One school's DIBELS-style goals (27 correct letter sounds/min at start of K, 43 by mid-year) are a rough illustration of the range, not an official benchmark ([school design doc](https://www.scribd.com/document/364242103/designdocumentfinal)).

**Session-level balance:**
- Review : new ratio about 80 : 20 by item count; this is what keeps first-attempt success near the DI 70-90% target.
- If a session's accuracy falls below 70%, the next session introduces nothing new and halves the item count.
- Hard cap 15 min; if the child wants more, offer a second *review-only* session later rather than extending.

### 10.3 Error-correction script (used everywhere)
1. Model: "That letter says /sh/." (immediate, calm, no buzzer)
2. Lead: "Say it with me: /sh/."
3. Test: "Your turn." (child alone)
4. Retest: same item returns 3-5 items later, then again at the end of the session.
Source: DI model-lead-test ([KU](https://specialconnections.ku.edu/instruction/direct_instruction); [Carnine et al.](https://newlearningonline.com/literacies/chapter-4/carnine-et-al.-on-direct-instruction)).

### 10.4 Content checklist for an 8-year-old
- Fonts: plain sans with single-storey *a* and *g* (what he will write); no decorative letterforms on the learning items.
- Audio: pure sounds, clipped stops, continuous sounds stretched; recorded by a human.
- No pictures that give the word away in reading steps; keyword pictures only in the "new sound" step.
- Mascot chosen by the child from 3-4 older-looking options; mascot "speaks in sounds".
- Decodable stories by Phase 3 should have a plot an 8-year-old would tolerate (missions, animals doing odd things, mild jokes), not nursery scenarios.
- Progress artefacts: sound map (44 cells), "words I can read" counter, streak, weekly fluency graph.
